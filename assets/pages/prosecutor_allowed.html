Central Issue

The State Had to Authenticate More Than a Screenshot

State’s Exhibit 4 was offered as a Facebook Messenger communication authored by Ben Allen. That required proof of two separate propositions: first, that the screenshot accurately reflected what appeared on Lauren Deal’s phone; and second, that Ben actually authored the specific message attributed to him.

Deal could identify what appeared on her own device. The harder question was authorship. The complete thread showed that another person, “Matt,” used Ben’s phone and identified himself within the same exchange. That fact did not conclusively prove Matt wrote the earlier message, but it defeated any assumption that the display name “Ben Allen” alone established who pressed send.

The State authenticated the image only marginally, did not adequately establish the witness’s personal basis for attributing the disputed message to Ben, failed to satisfy the factual condition imposed by the court, and then obtained a judicial statement to the jury that the evidence was “sufficiently reliable.”
What the Record Shows

The Sequence Matters

  1. Deal identified the exhibit as messages on her phone and agreed, through leading questions, that they were messages “between” her and Ben.
  2. The defense objected that the State had selected only one page even though later pages stated that “Matt” was using Ben’s phone and that Ben later denied possessing it.
  3. The judge acknowledged the third-party-access problem and required the State to connect the first message to a specific earlier bonfire discussion.
  4. Bartholomew did not clearly establish when, how, or with whom that prior bonfire discussion occurred.
  5. The judge nevertheless told the jury that the exhibit was admissible and “sufficiently reliable.”
  6. When the exhibit was published, the judge further described it as corroborative without explaining its limited purpose or resolving the authorship problem.

Rules 104(b), 901, and 602

Authentication Required Proof of Authorship, Not Merely Account Appearance

Rule 901 requires evidence sufficient to support a finding that an exhibit is what its proponent claims. For electronic communications offered as a defendant’s own statements, authentication must address both the accuracy of the captured image and the identity of the author.


Deal’s testimony that the screenshot came from her phone could authenticate the appearance of the screenshot. It did not automatically establish that Ben possessed his phone or authored the 1:49 p.m. message. Because the same thread showed another person using the device, the State needed additional circumstantial or direct evidence connecting Ben to that particular message.


The critical foundation should have included the facts underlying Deal’s conclusion: whether she had previously discussed that exact bonfire with Ben, when and how that discussion occurred, whether the content contained information uniquely known to him, whether he later acknowledged the message, and how she knew who controlled the phone at 1:49 p.m.

Rule 104(b)

The judge conditioned admission on proof of a prior bonfire conversation. Conditional relevance permits temporary admission only if the promised foundation is later supplied. If it is not supplied, the evidence should be excluded or stricken.

Rule 901(a)

A display name and a screenshot from the recipient’s phone are not necessarily enough when the record affirmatively shows third-party access to the sender’s device or account.

Rule 602

The witness needed a factual basis for knowing who authored the message. A bare answer that “Ben” sent it states the conclusion without establishing personal knowledge of device possession or authorship.

The Unmet Condition

Bartholomew Did Not Establish the Link the Judge Required

The judge twice stated that the State needed to link the screenshot to a conversation occurring before 1:49 p.m. on December 3, 2016. That condition was not advisory. It was the court’s stated basis for treating the bonfire reference as circumstantial evidence of authorship.


After the jury returned, Bartholomew established the date, timestamp, displayed sender, content of the message, Deal’s reply, and a later photograph of the bonfire. She did not clearly ask Deal whether she had previously discussed that specific bonfire with Ben, when the conversation occurred, how it occurred, or how she knew the earlier conversation was with him.


The later answer, “Way prior to this, yes,” concerned Facebook communication generally. It did not clearly establish the specific prior bonfire discussion the court identified as the necessary factual link. The State therefore substituted Deal’s conclusion—“Ben”—for the foundational facts the judge had required.


The Strongest Judicial Error

The Court Shifted the Burden and Then Vouched for the Exhibit

“There is no reason to believe that the first message was not from Ben Allen.”

That formulation inverted Rule 901. The defense did not bear the burden of proving that Ben did not send the message. The State, as proponent, had to introduce affirmative evidence sufficient to support a finding that he did. The correct question was whether the State produced a factual basis from which a reasonable juror could attribute the message to Ben—not whether the defense disproved authorship.

“I have overruled the objection and found proposed State’s Exhibit Number 4 to be admissible and sufficiently reliable to be admitted.”

The court could announce that the exhibit was admitted. It should not have told the jury that disputed evidence was “sufficiently reliable.” Reliability, authorship, credibility, and evidentiary weight were the contested factual questions. By using the court’s institutional authority to characterize the exhibit as reliable, the judge risked endorsing Deal’s attribution and the State’s theory in violation of N.C. Gen. Stat. § 15A-1222.


The later statement that the exhibit was admitted “for corroborative purposes” compounded the problem. Corroboration cannot substitute for authentication. Nor can the exhibit independently corroborate the witness’s authorship conclusion until authorship has first been adequately established.

Rule 106 and Rule 403

The State Presented the Page Most Favorable to Its Theory

The defense alerted the court that the State was offering only the first page while later pages disclosed third-party access and Ben’s denial of possession. Those later portions did not necessarily prove that Matt wrote the 1:49 p.m. message, but they were necessary to prevent the jury from treating the account name as proof of exclusive control and authorship.


Rule 106 permits the adverse party to require contemporaneous introduction of the portions necessary to prevent a misleading impression. Rule 403 separately supports exclusion where the probative value of an isolated page is substantially outweighed by the danger of confusion or misleading the jury.


The court offered to allow the defense to introduce the remaining pages. That partially recognized the problem, but it placed the burden of correcting the State’s selective presentation on the defense. The stronger request would have required the State to introduce the necessary context at the same time or forego the isolated page.


Best Preserved Objection

The Objection That Should Have Been Made Before Admission

Authentication, Personal Knowledge, Conditional Relevance, Completeness, and Rule 403


Objection under Rules 104(b), 901(a), 602, 106, and 403, and request for voir dire outside the jury’s presence. The witness has authenticated only that these screenshots appeared on her phone. She has not established personal knowledge that Mr. Allen authored the 1:49 p.m. message.


The complete conversation affirmatively shows that another person used the same phone or account, so the displayed name is not sufficient proof of authorship. The Court has conditioned admission on proof of a specific prior bonfire conversation with Mr. Allen, and the State has not supplied that foundation.


The State is also attempting to introduce only the first page while omitting the portions showing third-party access and Mr. Allen’s denial of possession. Under Rule 106, the necessary contextual portions must be introduced contemporaneously. Under Rule 403, the isolated page presents a substantial danger of misleading the jury.


The defense moves to exclude the exhibit. Alternatively, the defense moves to require introduction of the complete relevant exchange with an instruction that neither the account name nor admission of the exhibit establishes who authored any particular message.

Immediate Objection After the Court’s Reliability Statement

N.C. Gen. Stat. § 15A-1222; Curative Instruction; Mistrial if Necessary


Objection. The Court’s statement that the exhibit is “sufficiently reliable” expresses an opinion on disputed authorship, credibility, and evidentiary weight, which are questions for the jury under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 15A-1222.


The defense requests an immediate instruction that admission of the exhibit does not constitute a finding that Mr. Allen authored any message, that the Court expresses no opinion concerning the exhibit’s reliability or credibility, and that authorship and weight remain solely for the jury. If the prejudice cannot be cured, the defense moves for a mistrial under N.C. Gen. Stat. § 15A-1061.

Bottom Line

When The Prosecutor and the Judge Team Up

The State did not simply offer a screenshot. It offered the screenshot as Ben Allen’s own statement. Yet the complete thread disclosed third-party access; the State did not clearly establish the specific prior conversation the judge required, and the witness was permitted to state the ultimate conclusion of authorship without explaining how she knew it.


The judge then shifted the authentication burden by reasoning that there was no reason to believe Ben did not send the message. Most significantly, the judge told the jury that the exhibit was “sufficiently reliable,” placing judicial authority behind evidence whose authorship and weight remained disputed.


The strongest preserved issue would have combined Rules 104(b), 901, 602, 106, and 403 at admission, followed by an immediate § 15A-1222 objection, request for a curative instruction, and—if the prejudice could not be cured—a motion for mistrial under § 15A-1061.
