Scope and Method

What This Page Does—and Does Not Claim

This page reorganizes the supplied testimony analysis into a structured credibility and evidentiary issue map. It distinguishes direct contradictions from prior inconsistent statements, omissions, conflicts with other witnesses, limited corroboration, and biomechanical arguments.

Bottom Line: Any reader examining the record as a whole can clearly see it supports a coherent defense theory: the State’s case was built by selecting, reshaping, and advancing a usable story while the contemporaneous and objective evidence pointed in a different direction.

A direct contradiction can be stated firmly if the transcript and police interviews support both versions. An omission must be described as an omission. Unusual conduct is relevant to weight but is not proof of fabrication. A biomechanical argument can be powerful, but it should be demonstrated rather than asserted as scientific fact without expert or visual support, even when it is obvious, as it is in this case.

Emotional Testimony Was Used Where Factual Development Was Required

Rather than develop the missing physical facts, Bartholomew repeatedly directed the testimony toward Deal’s emotional experience: what was going through her mind, what she felt physically and emotionally, whether she expected the event, why she felt compelled to run, and how terrified she was.

Those feelings may have been relevant to Deal’s claimed freeze response or her decision to flee. They did not explain the mechanics of the alleged offense. Emotional testimony cannot supply the missing movements, body positions, timing, clothing-removal steps, or actions necessary to determine whether the physical sequence occurred as described.

The problem was therefore not merely a lack of detail. The prosecutor elicited an emotionally powerful conclusion while leaving the central physical account undeveloped. The jury was asked to accept that the sequence occurred without receiving the factual information necessary to evaluate how it occurred.

Emotion was used to reinforce the accusation, but it did not provide the missing proof.

The Caution Runs Both Ways—but the Burden Does Not

It is understood that the defense should not characterize the alleged sequence as definitively or scientifically impossible without testing, demonstrative evidence, or qualified expert analysis. The State, however, should not be permitted to treat the same untested sequence as physically coherent and proven merely because the complainant described it.

When the alleged mechanics are central to guilt and capable of objective examination, the prosecution should not avoid the factual questions necessary to determine whether the sequence could occur as described. The State may not substitute emotional testimony for proof of the event's physical mechanics and then place the consequences of that evidentiary gap on the Defendant.

The Defendant was not required to prove that the sequence was impossible. The burden remained on the State to prove the charged conduct beyond a reasonable doubt. If the prosecution did not investigate, test, explain, or meaningfully develop an account containing substantial spatial, timing, and movement problems, that failure properly weighs against the sufficiency and reliability of the State's evidence—not against the accused.

Evidentiary Significance

Rule 401 permits evidence that tends to make a consequential fact more or less probable. Emotional testimony may therefore have some relevance to fear, freezing, or flight. But its ability to prove the physical occurrence is limited.

Rule 403 permits exclusion when the probative value of evidence is substantially outweighed by the danger of unfair prejudice, confusion of the issues, or misleading the jury. Emotional testimony becomes especially problematic when it encourages the jury to treat the intensity of the witness's feelings as proof that an otherwise unexplained physical sequence occurred.

Rule 611(a) also required the court to control the examination so that the presentation remained effective for determining the truth. That authority supported limiting repetitive emotional questioning and requiring the State to focus on the disputed facts and mechanics of the alleged event.

It is not that every question concerning emotion was improper. Some of the questions had legitimate relevance. The point is that the emotional testimony had limited probative value regarding the physical occurrence and could not substitute for evidence explaining the charged conduct. North Carolina Evidence Framework

How the Issues Should Be Characterized Legally

Rule 607

Any party may attack a witness’s credibility, including the party that called the witness. This matters when the State’s own witnesses conflict.

Rule 613

Prior statements may be used to confront a witness with inconsistent accounts, subject to the rule’s procedural requirements and the distinction between impeachment and substantive use.

Rule 602

A witness must have personal knowledge of the matters described. The rule does not resolve credibility, but it limits testimony based only on inference or assumption.

Rules 401 and 403

Contradictions and omissions are relevant when they bear on consequential facts, but the court retains authority to prevent confusion, unfair prejudice, or misleading use.

The “inherently incredible” doctrine is narrow

North Carolina law draws a line between two different claims:

Jury argument / inherent incredibility:
A fact-finder is entitled to reject testimony that describes a physical sequence that cannot occur as claimed. When the witness herself accepts continuous one-handed control of both arms before and during the removal of tight leggings, the removal of shoes, the defendant’s undressing, the forced separation of thighs, and penetration, the geometric conflict is patent.

Pants cannot pass through the space occupied by a body that is already between the legs. That is a powerful, record-based argument that the account is inherently incredible. It can and should be pressed hard in cross-examination, closing, and post-trial motions.

Claim of legal error requiring exclusion or directed verdict as a matter of law:
Courts are extremely reluctant to take the issue away from the jury merely because the described sequence appears impossible. Absent (a) uncontradicted physical facts or demonstrative evidence that affirmatively disprove the sequence, or (b) a clear, contemporaneous objection that forced the trial court to confront the impossibility and rule on it, appellate courts treat the problem as one of weight and credibility.
